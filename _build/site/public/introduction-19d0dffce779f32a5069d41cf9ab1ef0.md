# Introduction

On the evening of April 14, 1865, the U.S President Abraham Lincoln, walked into Ford’s Theatre in Washington, D.C.

He had made decisions under uncertainty his entire adult life. He had navigated war, political collapse, incomplete information, and irreversible consequences. He understood risk. He understood stakes. He understood that action often precedes clarity.

What he did not know—what he could not know—was how that particular evening would unfold.

From his perspective, the future contained multiple possible paths. Most were ordinary. Some were favorable. And, as we now know, one was catastrophic. No amount of intelligence, experience, or moral seriousness could collapse those possibilities into a single, certain outcome before the moment arrived.

That kind of uncertainty is extreme, but it is not unique.

The world is not orderly in the way our minds would like it to be.

Events happen without warning. Outcomes vary even when conditions look identical. Effort does not reliably map onto reward. Patterns appear, disappear, and reappear just long enough to fool us into believing we’ve understood what’s going on.

If you are young, inexperienced, or lack understanding about how the world works, you'll think of this as a temporary problem caused by ignorance or lack of data. But, that's not what is happening behind the curtain of everyday life.

Chaos, expressed as variation of outcomes in life is a permanent feature of reality. The world is permeated by randomness.

I know, it's not fair. If you put the same amount of effort into something as the next guy, you'd expect the same reward. And you'd get upset if that doesn't happen.

Consider a familiar scenario.

Two people graduate in the same year, from similar schools, with similar grades. They apply to the same kinds of jobs. One quickly lands a well-paid position, builds momentum, and within a few years looks “successful.” The other gets rejected again and again, takes temporary work, and starts to doubt their own ability.

From the inside, this may feel like a verdict on talent, effort, character, or the society. Some people blame themselves; others, the government, corporations, the media, etc.

But step back, and a uncomfortable, yet profound picture appears: small differences early on—who saw which posting, who happened to interview on a good day, who got lucky with timing—can compound into radically different outcomes.

When scientists say a process is _noisy_, they mean something exactly like this:  
outcomes depend not only on the things that _should_ matter, but also on many small, uncontrollable factors that vary from case to case.

The concept of _noise_ will play an important concept in this book, so it's good if you familiarize youself with it right at the outset. The word "noise" is related to sound, but it adopts a wider meaning. If you're listening to your favorite podcast and suddenly the clarity of the host's voice scrambles under some interference (say you stand next to some other electronic device), than such interference is called noise.

This usage comes originally from the information science, where it is understood as random effect of the unknown sources in the environment on the content of the message being transferred through some channel. What noise interferes with is called the _signal_. The voice on the podcast is the signal you want to hear, and noise prevents that.

Now, we can generalize this idea of noise and apply it to any other context in which information is exchanged. If you're looking outside of your window on a foggy day and you can't clearly see whether your favorite pizza place is open or not, then fog could be considered a noise obscuring the signal: the "Open" sign on the door of the pizzeria.

Or, if you want to take a class with some professor but their student reviews contain both stellar and not so stellar feedback, then the variability in the reviews could be considered a noise obscuring the signal - the truth about the quality of that professor.

So, noise could be understood as the existence of variablity in reality that clouds our efforts at knowing what is true. It is randomness layered on top of structure of reality.

At this point, it helps to connect a few terms that will appear throughout the book.

- _Randomness_ refers to the fact that outcomes vary even when conditions look similar.

- _Noise_ refers to how that randomness interferes with our ability to see what matters (the truth).

- _Uncertainty_ is what we experience when we have to act without knowing which outcome will occur.

These ideas overlap, and that overlap is intentional. In this book, we will discuss all three.

For you, right now, the main task is to understand and accept these three mutually related concepts as inseparable aspects of our reality. They can be further summarized like this:

- Randomness exists in the world.

- Noise affects what we can know about it.

- Uncertainty is what remains when we have to decide anyway.

The tricky thing with them is that they are often unavoidable. There is alwas some amount of noise in any environment. It can never be fully eliminated. Randomness affects everything, and uncertainty is the default state of being.

This is not limited to careers or effort. It applies literally to _everything_ in life.

A video posted online can receive ten views or ten million, even when the quality difference is small. A dating profile can succeed or fail depending on timing, mood, or the order in which it is seen. A startup can collapse or explode based on conditions that no one could fully predict in advance.

The numbers we often obsess over—-grades, rankings, scores, follower counts, swipe-lefts-—suggest that we can achieve absolute precision in life.

But that's an illusion. The processes that generate those numbers often escape precision.

## The real task of critical thinking

Critical thinking is often described as “thinking clearly,” “avoiding fallacies,” or “being logical.” Those descriptions are not wrong, but they miss something more fundamental.

At its core, critical thinking is the attempt to **reduce uncertainty as much as reality allows**—and no more.

I want you to pay attention to this definition of critical thinking, because it points out to a few very important details.

First, it is very humble. It doesn't say that uncertainty can be eliminated. It accepts that there is an irreducible amount of noise (as variability and uncertainty) in life, and that sometimes we can't do much about it.

Second, it doesn't want to deny uncertainty, or pretend that it's not there. That would be very unproductive. We live in the real world and we should accept its features, no matter what they are. Reality doesn't care about our feelings.

Finally, it suggests that our job as critical thinkers is to reduce this uncertainty as much as it is really possible.

We neither escape from uncertainty, nor allow it to prevent us from living our life to the fullest. Instead, we treat it as a constraint, or an obstacle, to be managed intelligently.

I want to stress another important aspect of what this book will attempt to teach you. The point of reducing uncertainty is not to achieve more comfort or increase our knowledge. While those might be important on their own, that's not what critical thinking is really about.

For me, critical thinking is about _action_.

Every real decision is made before uncertainty disappears. Waiting for perfect clarity usually means not acting at all, or acting too late. Acting blindly, on the other hand, means mistaking confidence for understanding.

Critical thinking occupies the space in between.

It asks: given how much uncertainty is unavoidable here, what kind of action still makes sense?

Although I am a philosophy professor (and my kind has been known to indulge in reading and knowledge collection, sometimes excessively), I don't want to encourage you to bury your head in a library and become a brainiac.

I understand you might have different goals in life, and some of them demand that you _act_ on something before you fully understand it. So, I want to encourage you do **act as much as you can**. Do things in life, make mistakes, risk. These are important.

But if you want to _act smart_, then this book is for you. So, continue reading.

## Science as a discipline of uncertainty reduction

Science is often misunderstood as a machine for producing certainty. But it isn’t. Any easy way to recognize a person who does not really know how science works is by seeing how they interpret scientific work and its outcomes. The fact that some people get a bad reaction to some vaccine doesn't mean that scientists are lying to you that they work. Noise occurs everywhere, even in medical science. No scientist worth their salt will tell you anything different.

However, science is the most successful **practice of uncertainty management** humans have ever developed. Its power does not come from claiming absolute truth, but from learning how to ask questions in ways that make error visible, measurable, and correctable.

Crucially, science also knows its limits.

No scientific claim is perfectly certain. No measurement is noise-free. No prediction is immune to surprise.

And yet, science works, precisely because it takes uncertainty seriously rather than personally.

If we want to be good critical thinkers in everyday life, we cannot ignore these lessons. We must borrow them.

## Why uncertainty has shapes

You may have noticed the book's title and wondered how uncertainty can have a 'shape'.

What's behind it is the understanding that not all uncertainty is the same.

For example, some uncertain situations are flat and indifferent. Others clusters around averages. Some hide danger in rare extremes. Others grows worse over time. Some of them reset and forget the past entirely.

Treating all uncertainty as if it were identical is one of the most common—and most costly—thinking errors people make.

The way you reason about:

- waiting in a line,
- evaluating medical risks,
- interpreting success and failure,
- planning a career,
- reacting to news and statistics,

depends on **what kind of uncertainty you are facing**.

And different kinds of uncertainty require different mental tools.

## What this book is—and what it is not

This book is not a science or a statistics textbook. You will find no formulas here; there is no math to memorize.

Instead, you should understand this textbook as a **philosophical field guide** through life and the myriad of uncertain situations you will face, almost on a daily basis.

Each chapter introduces a _shape of uncertainty_—a recurring pattern that appears in science, economics, biology, technology, and everyday life. You will meet these shapes first through familiar situations, not abstract definitions. Only after your intuitive understanding is clear will we talk about it in more scientific and statistical terms.

So, the goal here is not calculation. I am not a math teacher and I will not bother you with those. My goal is for you to understand the most powerful statistical concepts scientists use in their work so you could apply them in your everyday life.

Understanding these _shapes of uncertainty_ will help you:

- avoid predictable reasoning traps,
- make better decisions under imperfect information,
- and stop demanding certainty where none is available.

## How to read this book

To get the most of the content here, read it with an open mind and try to think about your life and experience as you process the concepts. Some of them might be easy to understand; others will be harder. But all of them will apply to some aspect of reality you have, or will in due time, experienced.

The main aim of the bnook is to help you gain **a clearer view of the limits within which thinking and action are possible.**

That clarity will change how you move through the world.

It will not tell you what will happen; but it will help you choose what to do anyway.

That is the beginning of real critical thinking.
