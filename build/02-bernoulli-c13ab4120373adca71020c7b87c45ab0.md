# Yes and No Worlds

:class: key-idea

- Why many real-life uncertainties collapse into binary outcomes

- Why streaks feel informative here—but often mislead
- When repetition does matter, and when it still doesn’t
- How binary thinking distorts judgment in life, work, and morality

Knowing how to make distinctions between two things is a fundamental human ability. Even newborns have it; fresh out of the womb, they can distinguish between everything that is their mother and that isn't.

Because it's so basic, it has become an integral part of how we perceive the world. Many outcomes in our life boil down to this binary choices: yes and no, pass and fail, accept and reject, and many others.

You probably recognize this in yourself. We humans are an evaluative creatures. We love giving value judgments that collapse into a binary choice. True and false, success and failure, allowed and denied. Outcomes such as these feel decisive and meaningful. They simplify our cognitive tasks and make reasoning easier.

This is particularly useful when we're observing a process and wish to know whether something is true of the process itself, not its individual outcomes. Think about the flipping the coin. A fair coin will, on average, have a roughly equal amount of heads and tails. If you're testing the fairness of the coin, a long streak of heads will make you raise your eyebrows and you'd naturally be suspicious.

Same goes for other binary outcomes. If the government is using lottery to choose the recipient of some welfare, if a same person receives it several times in a row, you'll assume the entire process is not really kosher: something is happening in the background.

All of this is because streaks of singular outcomes are a signal to you that there is a certain pattern shaping the random process you're observing.

:::{admonition} Key idea
:class: key-idea

Binary-based processes are everywhere.

:::

However, how many much deviation of the outcomes of such a process from a roughly equal distribution warrant this kind of judgment? If you flip the coin ten times and all ten are heads? What if seven of them are heads, and three are tails? Where should we draw the line?

Knowing more about binary types of uncertainty can help us understand this. This is important because binary uncertainty can trick us easily into believing something is happening when it's actually not.

## When the world forces a choice

Binary outcomes do not exist in nature. Reality is complex and almost everything admits to more than two simple choices. Even the coin flip, the simplest example of binary outcomes is not _actually_ binary. The coin is three-dimensional, and there's nothing that physically prevents the coin from landng on the edge. If you include the rotation, there are almost infinite number of positions it could land after the flip.

But those other possibilities are so rare that we ignore them. We merely _assume_ those other possibilities don't exist so we could reason about it and make some predictions.

Similar things are happening when we consider other binary choices. Take passing or failing the exam. In general, when a student takes the test, he or she gets a score on some continuous scale (say percentage of correct answers) and if the test has more than just a few questions, or if it admits partial answers, then the student could get _any_ score between 0 and 100. However, the teacher _collapses_ the gradation of the score into a threshold: if the student has more than, say 60% or more correct answers, she passes; otherwise, she fails.

You can see that there is a lot of arbitrariness in this system. If Bob gets 59% and Anne gets 60% she passes, and he fails. The difference between them is minuscule; yet the boundary must be drawn somewhere. If Bob asks the teacher to give him a pass, and the teaches does that, then he exposes herself from another request: if Jane got 58% and fails, when she sees Bob getting a pass for 59%, she could make the same argument as Bob and pressure the teacher to lower the grade. As you can see, such an approach would be unsustainable.

All of this is to say that forcing the world to give us a binary answer is a conscious decisions by thinkers who wish to understand the world in more detail and not get bogged down by infinite-regress of adjustment. It doesn't mean believing that the world is always very simple (we know it is not), but this simplicity sometimes needs to be forced so we could act in the world.

:::{admonition} Pitfall: Binaries are man-made
:class: pitfall

The world itself is not binary, but continuous. We impose binary outcomes to simplify it.

:::

Besides coin flips and college exams, these kinds of 'collapses' happen in many areas of life, from medicine (where some tests are reduced to pass or fail), online content moderation, instututional approvals, various admission processes, etc.

## The shape of binary uncertainty

The core idea of the binary uncertainty is the reduction of some random process to only two outcomes. What these two outcomes are is up to humans to decide, depending on the nature of the random process.

Unlike uniform randomness, the binary one is explicitly _labelled_: we have specific terms for the outcomes. Where the uniform randomness could admit a near infinite (or actually infinite) number of outcomes (imagine drawing a single natural number) the binary one never has more than two.

Each of the outcomes has an associated _probability_ of occurring. You can understand this either as a frequency (the ratio of the successes--the number of times this outcome occurred--and the total number of all possible outcomes--just two in this case), or the propensity, the tendency for some outcome to occurr more than the others.

For example, the probablility of flipping heads on a fair coin is 0.5 (or 50%). We know that because if we flip one 1,000 times, we'll roughly get half of them heads and half tails.

Probability theory comes with certain rules that help us make the most sense out of uncertain processes. One of those rules is that the sum of probabilities of all possible outcomes in some random process (this is called 'the outcome space') will sum up to 1. For example, in the coin flip, we only have two outcomes, and the probabilities of heads and tails (0.5 for each) sum up to one.

If we're tossing a fair die, then the outcome space has six possible outcomes, and since (per uniform probability distribution discussed in the last chapter) all of them are equally likely, then we get their probabilities by dividing 1 by 6: the result is 0.16, approximately.

This is useful because once we know the probability of one outcome, we can immediately know the probability of the other too. If the coin is rigged to give tails 70% of the time (if the probability of heads is 0.7), then we know that the probability of heads is 0.3 (because 1 - 0.7 = 0.3).

Binary uncertainty can be used to understand single events, such as lottery draws, or coin flips, or football game bets, as well as continuous processes consisting of the repetition of some binary events, such as shots on a basketball hoop.

When we consider a single event, we talk about something called the **Bernoulli distribution**, named by a famous Swiss scientist, Jacob Bernoulli. When we apply this to a continuous process, then we call it **Binomial distribution** (where 'binomial' simply means 'two names').

## How to avoid being fooled by binary uncertainty

Let’s take a familiar situation. Imagine you are shooting a basketball ten times. You are not perfect, but you are decent. On average, you make about 70% of your shots.

What should you expect to happen?

Most people answer this too quickly. They say: seven shots will go in. And that sounds reasonable. But it is also misleading.

Because the question “What should I expect?” has two very different meanings.

One meaning is about long-run averages. If you repeated this experiment many times—ten shots today, ten tomorrow, ten the day after—then yes, across all those attempts, about 70% of the shots would go in.

But the other meaning is about what actually happens in one concrete run. And here, binary uncertainty behaves very differently.

In a single set of ten shots, many outcomes are possible.

You might score all ten, or none, or seven, eight, or any number in this range. And binary uncertainty helps us understand that all of these are compatible with the same underlying ability. That's simply the nature of this process. No matter one's skill, randomness affects everything, not just in basketball games but in every other aspect of life.

This is the first place where binary uncertainty fools us: we confuse expected proportions with expected experiences. If somebody usually scores 70% of his shots, that doesn't mean he will score (or will not score) on any particular attempt. These are averages of the process, not single events.

#### Why extremes can happen without meaning anything

Now suppose that one day you score all ten shots.

How do you feel?

If you are like most people, you feel that something important has been revealed. You might think you are “on fire,” that you have improved, that today is special. You may even change how you act next—take harder shots, trust your hand more, infer momentum.

But the truth is that scoring all ten shots is not evidence that your probability suddenly became 100%.

It is an allowed outcome of a 70% process.

It is unlikely, but unlikely does not mean impossible. When you repeat binary events a finite number of times, unlikely outcomes must happen.

The same is true at the other extreme. You might miss all ten shots and start thinking you're a worthless athlete. But it does not prove that your true ability is zero.

When dealing with randomness, the main trick is to distinguish between singal and noise; that is, between meaningful outcomes revealing some underlying truth and those influenced by the random processes. The first ones are 'diagnostic': they give us useful information about reality, while others do not. Instead, they confuse us and make us believe things that are not true.

Binary uncertainty regularly produces outcomes that feel diagnostic but are not.

So, to avoid being fooled by a binary randomness, instead of asking “what happened this time,” we should ask:

“What range of outcomes does this process naturally generate?”

If your probability of success is 70%, then the set of ten-shot outcomes forms a **distribution**. Most of the time, you will land near seven successes. Sometimes you will get eight or nine. Sometimes five or six. And very rarely, you will hit the extremes—zero or ten.

That's why the grap shows a 'hump' and not a flat row of equally sized bars that we saw in uniform randomness in the last chapter. That hump indicates the truth behind the process: where do the scores have a a tendency to cluster, and what are the most likely outcomes. Seven and other outcomes around it are most common, while others on either extreme taper off. They do exist, but don't occur that often.

The guiding principle for binary uncertainty is that we should not reason from a single sequence when the process is probabilistic. A single run is never a diagnosis, as much as a one streak is not a revelation. We must have patience and wisdom to look at the ensembles and distill the truth from the entire distribution.

## The properties of binary uncertainty

Let’s now slow down and examine what this kind of uncertainty looks like when viewed through the same lens we used before.

#### Typicality

In binary uncertainty, there are only two possible results. Since we can have any possible distribution between their probabilities, there is an infinite number of typicalities. For example, heads could have probability, 0.5, 0.49, 0.48, 0.479999, or any other number, and tails would be just the difference between 1 and that number.

But in each distrbution of the probabilities between the two, there will be a single ratio that will tell us what is the exact typicallity of each situation.

#### Extremeness

Binary uncertainty does not contain extreme values in the usual sense. There is no outcome that is inherently rare or shocking. Both outcomes are always on the table.

What could be extreme are long streaks: many successes in a row, or many failures without interruption. These sequences may be revealing something about the underlying reality, but we have to be careful not to take any particular sequence as meaningful. If you flip a coin 1 million times, it is possible to get many 10-heads or 10-tails sequences while the coin may not be biased at all.

This is one of the most persistent traps in binary reasoning: treating local extremeness as informative.

#### Memory

Most binary processes are memoryless.

Each trial stands on its own. The coin does not remember what it landed on last time. The approval committee does not feel obligated to balance yesterday’s decision. The random draw does not compensate for past outcomes. If you don't win the lottery today, it doesn't mean you will win it tomorrow.

We often fall into the memory trap. Our minds do remember, and we keep score expecting that things will happen one way just because we observed them happening certain way in the past.

This mismatch—between a memoryless process and a memory-rich observer—is a major source of error.

#### Accumulation

Outcomes in binary uncertainty accumulate only as counts.

Ten successes do not push the system toward an eleventh. Ten failures do not build pressure for reversal. Nothing “adds up” in the sense of increasing future likelihood.

Accumulation here is descriptive, not causal. It summarizes what has happened, but it does not influence what will happen next.

This is deeply unintuitive, especially in domains where effort usually compounds. Binary uncertainty strips that intuition of its power.

#### Predictive horizon

Binary uncertainty is opaque at the level of individual events. It contains nothing but a thick fog that occludes any attempt to discover the underlying truth.

This means that we cannot reliably predict the next outcome, no matter how many times the process has already occurred.

However, over many repetitions, proportions stabilize, and frequencies settle. The process reveals its long-run tendencies, but never its next move.

This creates a dangerous illusion: when we understand the long-term propensity, we may fall into a trap of believing that we have the power to know the outcome of the next event. But, that never happens.

#### Action implications

This doesn't mean that all action in the face of such uncertainty is impossible. Even if we can't know the outcome of the next event, knowing something about the underlying process can give us useful and actionable information that could help us benefit.

For example, if you knew that some coin is rigged to give heads 70% of the time, you should not gamble your entire life-savings on the result of the next flip, but you definitely should accept some smaller repetitive gambles with the same coin. If you played 100 games with a /$1 bet per flip, you'd expect to win /$70.

## Binary uncertainty in real life

If you observe and think about the world a bit, you will see that binary uncertainty is everywhere. That's because it feels so natural to us.

We encounter it any time a complex process is collapsed into a yes-or-no outcome. The world may be continuous underneath, but what we experience is a gate: pass or fail, accepted or rejected, visible or invisible.

Consider job or college applications. Behind every decision there is a long chain of evaluations, comparisons, trade-offs, and constraints. Committees debate. Reviewers disagree. External factors intervene. But what reaches you is a single bit of information: yes or no. Offer or rejection. Funded or not funded.

When people apply repeatedly and receive several rejections in a row, they often conclude that something fundamental must be wrong with them. They read streaks as diagnoses. But in many of these systems, each application is judged independently, often under shifting conditions. A string of “no” outcomes does not necessarily indicate decline, nor does a sudden “yes” signal a permanent change in status. The binary nature of the outcome tempts us to over-interpret what is actually a noisy process.

Dating and social feedback works in a similar way. Messages get replied to or ignored. Matches happen or they don’t. Interest is expressed or withheld. The underlying reasons are often foggy and contingent—timing, mood, context, competing attention—but what you experience is a clean split. Over time, people start constructing narratives around streaks of rejection or acceptance, even when nothing systematic has changed about them or their behavior.

Creative work and publishing are another domain where binary uncertainty dominates experience. An article is accepted or rejected. A TikTok video is promoted or buried. A book gets picked up for publishing or passed over.

From the outside, these outcomes look diagnostic. From the inside, they often depend on thresholds, quotas, editorial taste, algorithmic filters, and chance exposure. Many creators on social media, like TikTok, Instagram, or YouTube, are haunted by the feeling that repeated failure to get likes or views must “mean something,” when in fact they are sampling repeatedly from a process that does not accumulate memory in the way they imagine.

Medical testing and screening adds a darker edge to this pattern. Tests are frequently reported as positive or negative, normal or abnormal. Yet most medical indicators live on continuous scales. Thresholds are imposed for practical reasons: to guide intervention, to manage risk, to standardize decisions, or some other practical reason. A result just above a cutoff can feel catastrophic, while one just below can feel reassuring, even though the underlying difference may be negligible, like the grade percentage between Bob and Anne. The binary framing amplifies emotional response while hiding an entire gradation beneath.

Immigration and legal decisions may be the most consequential examples of all. Applications are approved or denied. Status is granted or revoked. Families are separated or allowed to stay together. Behind these outcomes lie long procedural chains, discretionary judgments, and bureaucratic constraints. But for the person affected, everything collapses into a single bit of information that determines years of their life.

Across all of these domains, the challenge to our critical thinking is the same.

Binary outcomes invite moral interpretation. They tempt us to see desert, failure, worth, or intent where there may only be thresholds, quotas, and probabilistic filtering. They encourage us to believe that repetition must reveal progress or decline, even when each trial is effectively a fresh draw.

Understanding binary uncertainty does not make rejection painless or success trivial. But it does prevent a specific kind of error: mistaking a sequence of yes-or-no outcomes for a narrative about who you are or how the world fundamentally works.

Sometimes, what feels like judgment is just a gate.

:class: exercise

Think of one situation in your own life that produced a clear yes/no outcome.

Choose something that mattered to you. Avoid games or lotteries. Use a real decision.

Examples (choose one, or supply your own):
• a job, grant, or school application
• a message that was answered or ignored
• a piece of work that was accepted or rejected
• a medical test result
• a platform decision (visibility, moderation, approval)

**Step 1** — Describe the situation (4–5 lines)

What was the outcome?
What action did you take to trigger it?
How much control did you believe you had?

**Step 2** — Classify the uncertainty

Answer each briefly, even if the answer feels uncomfortable.
• Binary collapse: What continuous process was reduced to yes/no?
• Memory: Did past attempts actually change future chances, or did each attempt reset?
• Typicality: Did it make sense to talk about “what usually happens” here?
• Extremeness: Were rare outcomes important, or just emotionally loud?

**Step 3** — Streak audit

Did you experience (or imagine) a streak?

If yes:
• What meaning did you assign to it at the time?
• What alternative explanation fits the structure better?

If no:
• What streak would have alarmed you?
• Would that alarm have been justified?

**Step 4** — Action check

Given the structure you identified:
• Was persistence rational?
• Was withdrawal rational?
• Was interpretation doing more work than evidence?
