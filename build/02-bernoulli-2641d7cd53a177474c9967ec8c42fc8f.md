Knowing how to make distinctions between two things is a fundamental human ability. Even newborns have it; fresh out of the womb, they can distinguish between everything that is their mother and that isn't.

Because it's so basic, it has become an integral part of how we perceive the world. Many outcomes in our life boil down to this binary choices: yes and no, pass and fail, accept and reject, and many others.

You probably recognize this in yourself. We humans are an evaluative creatures. We love giving value judgments that collapse into a binary choice. True and false, success and failure, allowed and denied. Outcomes such as these feel decisive and meaningful. They simplify our cognitive tasks and make reasoning easier.

This is particularly useful when we're observing a process and wish to know whether something is true of the process itself, not its individual outcomes. Think about the flipping the coin. A fair coin will, on average, have a roughly equal amount of heads and tails. If you're testing the fairness of the coin, a long streak of heads will make you raise your eyebrows and you'd naturally be suspicious.

Same goes for other binary outcomes. If the government is using lottery to choose the recipient of some welfare, if a same person receives it several times in a row, you'll assume the entire process is not really kosher: something is happening in the background.

All of this is because streaks of singular outcomes are a signal to you that there is a certain pattern shaping the random process you're observing.

However, how many much deviation of the outcomes of such a process from a roughly equal distribution warrant this kind of judgment? If you flip the coin ten times and all ten are heads? What if seven of them are heads, and three are tails? Where should we draw the line?

Knowing more about binary types of uncertainty can help us understand this. This is important because binary uncertainty can trick us easily into believing something is happening when it's actually not.

## When the world forces a choice

Binary outcomes do not exist in nature. Reality is complex and almost everything admits to more than two simple choices. Even the coin flip, the simplest example of binary outcomes is not _actually_ binary. The coin is three-dimensional, and there's nothing that physically prevents the coin from landng on the edge. If you include the rotation, there are almost infinite number of positions it could land after the flip.

But those other possibilities are so rare that we ignore them. We merely _assume_ those other possibilities don't exist so we could reason about it and make some predictions.

Similar things are happening when we consider other binary choices. Take passing or failing the exam. In general, when a student takes the test, he or she gets a score on some continuous scale (say percentage of correct answers) and if the test has more than just a few questions, or if it admits partial answers, then the student could get _any_ score between 0 and 100. However, the teacher _collapses_ the gradation of the score into a threshold: if the student has more than, say 60% or more correct answers, she passes; otherwise, she fails.

You can see that there is a lot of arbitrariness in this system. If Bob gets 59% and Anne gets 60% she passes, and he fails. The difference between them is minuscule; yet the boundary must be drawn somewhere. If Bob asks the teacher to give him a pass, and the teaches does that, then he exposes herself from another request: if Jane got 58% and fails, when she sees Bob getting a pass for 59%, she could make the same argument as Bob and pressure the teacher to lower the grade. As you can see, such an approach would be unsustainable.

All of this is to say that forcing the world to give us a binary answer is a conscious decisions by thinkers who wish to understand the world in more detail and not get bogged down by infinite-regress of adjustment. It doesn't mean believing that the world is always very simple (we know it is not), but this simplicity sometimes needs to be forced so we could act in the world.

Besides coin flips and college exams, these kinds of 'collapses' happen in many areas of life, from medicine (where some tests are reduced to pass or fail), online content moderation, instututional approvals, various admission processes, etc.

## The shape of binary uncertainty

The core idea of the binary uncertainty is the reduction of some random process to only two outcomes. What these two outcomes are is up to humans to decide, depending on the nature of the random process.

Unlike uniform randomness, the binary one is explicitly _labelled_: we have specific terms for the outcomes. Where the uniform randomness could admit a near infinite (or actually infinite) number of outcomes (imagine drawing a single natural number) the binary one never has more than two.

Each of the outcomes has an associated _probability_ of occurring. You can understand this either as a frequency (the ratio of the successes--the number of times this outcome occurred--and the total number of all possible outcomes--just two in this case), or the propensity, the tendency for some outcome to occurr more than the others.

For example, the probablility of flipping heads on a fair coin is 0.5 (or 50%). We know that because if we flip one 1,000 times, we'll roughly get half of them heads and half tails.

Probability theory comes with certain rules that help us make the most sense out of uncertain processes. One of those rules is that the sum of probabilities of all possible outcomes in some random process (this is called 'the outcome space') will sum up to 1. For example, in the coin flip, we only have two outcomes, and the probabilities of heads and tails (0.5 for each) sum up to one.

If we're tossing a fair die, then the outcome space has six possible outcomes, and since (per uniform probability distribution discussed in the last chapter) all of them are equally likely, then we get their probabilities by dividing 1 by 6: the result is 0.16, approximately.

This is useful because once we know the probability of one outcome, we can immediately know the probability of the other too. If the coin is rigged to give tails 70% of the time (if the probability of heads is 0.7), then we know that the probability of heads is 0.3 (because 1 - 0.7 = 0.3).

Binary uncertainty can be used to understand single events, such as lottery draws, or coin flips, or football game bets, as well as continuous processes consisting of the repetition of some binary events, such as shots on a basketball hoop.

When we consider a single event, we talk about something called the **Bernoulli distribution**, named by a famous Swiss scientist, Jacob Bernoulli. When we apply this to a continuous process, then we call it **Binomial distribution** (where 'binomial' simply means 'two names').

## How to avoid being fooled by binary uncertainty

Let’s take a familiar situation. Imagine you are shooting a basketball ten times. You are not perfect, but you are decent. On average, you make about 70% of your shots.

What should you expect to happen?

Most people answer this too quickly. They say: seven shots will go in. And that sounds reasonable. But it is also misleading.

Because the question “What should I expect?” has two very different meanings.

One meaning is about long-run averages. If you repeated this experiment many times—ten shots today, ten tomorrow, ten the day after—then yes, across all those attempts, about 70% of the shots would go in.

But the other meaning is about what actually happens in one concrete run. And here, binary uncertainty behaves very differently.

In a single set of ten shots, many outcomes are possible.

You might score all ten, or none, or seven, eight, or any number in this range. And binary uncertainty helps us understand that all of these are compatible with the same underlying ability. Why extremes can happen without meaning anything

Now suppose that one day you score all ten shots.

How do you feel?

If you are like most people, you feel that something important has been revealed. You might think you are “on fire,” that you have improved, that today is special. You may even change how you act next—take harder shots, trust your hand more, infer momentum.

But here is the uncomfortable fact: scoring all ten shots is not evidence that your probability suddenly became 100%.

It is an allowed outcome of a 70% process.

It is unlikely, yes—but unlikely does not mean impossible. And when you repeat binary events a finite number of times, unlikely outcomes are not just possible; they are inevitable.

The same is true at the other extreme. You might miss all ten shots. That feels catastrophic. It invites self-doubt, panic, or radical adjustment. But it does not prove that your true ability is zero.

Binary uncertainty regularly produces outcomes that feel diagnostic but are not.

Seeing the full shape instead of isolated outcomes

This is where a crucial shift in perspective matters.

Instead of asking:

“What happened this time?”

we should ask:

“What range of outcomes does this process naturally generate?”

If your probability of success is 70%, then the set of ten-shot outcomes forms a distribution. Most of the time, you will land near seven successes. Sometimes you will get eight or nine. Sometimes five or six. And very rarely, you will hit the extremes—zero or ten.

Those extremes do not dominate. They do not persist. They do not repeat reliably.

But they do occur.

And when they occur, they feel louder than they are.

This is why binary uncertainty is so psychologically treacherous. It compresses reality into yes/no outcomes, then tempts us to treat each sequence as a verdict on the underlying process.

What the graph would show you

If you were to plot all possible outcomes of ten shots with a 70% success rate, you would not see a flat line.

You would see a hump.

Outcomes near seven would be most common. Outcomes further away would taper off. The extremes—zero or ten—would appear as tiny bars at the edges.

They exist. They are real. But they are rare.

Seeing this shape matters because it teaches a discipline of interpretation: extreme outcomes should be placed, not privileged.

They belong to the distribution, not above it.

The practical rule

Here is the guiding principle for binary uncertainty:

Do not reason from a single sequence when the process is probabilistic.

One run is not a diagnosis.
One streak is not a revelation.
One collapse is not a trend.

Binary processes reward patience in interpretation, not intensity of reaction.

To avoid being fooled, you must resist the urge to treat outcomes as messages. In binary uncertainty, outcomes are often just outcomes.

Meaning lives in the pattern across repetitions—not in the drama of any particular run.

## The properties of binary uncertainty

Let’s now slow down and examine what this kind of uncertainty looks like when viewed through the same lens we used before.

### Typicality (and favoritism)

In binary uncertainty, the distinction between a favored outcome and a typical outcome collapses almost entirely.

There are only two possible results. If one of them happens more often than the other, then it is both more likely and more representative. There is no deeper structure hiding behind that fact.

If a coin lands heads 60% of the time, then heads is the typical outcome. It is also the favored one. There is no additional notion of “centrality” beyond frequency itself.

This is why binary processes are so tempting to over-interpret. In small samples, random variation can easily produce imbalance. A few successes in a row start to feel meaningful. A few failures begin to feel ominous. Our minds rush to label one outcome as dominant long before the evidence warrants it.

The important lesson here is that typicality in binary uncertainty is not something you see—it is something that only emerges through repetition.

Extremeness

Binary uncertainty does not contain extreme values in the usual sense.

There is no outcome that is inherently rare or shocking. Both outcomes are always on the table.

What does feel extreme are long streaks: many successes in a row, or many failures without interruption. These sequences trigger strong reactions in us. They feel exceptional, even unnatural.

But the extremeness here belongs to the sequence, not to the outcomes themselves. A streak does not signal that the process has changed. It only reflects how randomness sometimes clusters.

This is one of the most persistent traps in binary reasoning: treating emotional extremeness as informational extremeness.

Memory

Most binary processes are memoryless.

Each trial stands on its own. The coin does not remember what it landed on last time. The approval committee does not feel obligated to balance yesterday’s decision. The random draw does not compensate for past outcomes.

Yet our minds do remember. We keep score. We narrate streaks. We feel momentum or doom where none exists.

This mismatch—between a memoryless process and a memory-rich observer—is a major source of error.

Accumulation

Outcomes in binary uncertainty accumulate only as counts.

Ten successes do not push the system toward an eleventh. Ten failures do not build pressure for reversal. Nothing “adds up” in the sense of increasing future likelihood.

Accumulation here is descriptive, not causal. It summarizes what has happened, but it does not influence what will happen next.

This is deeply unintuitive, especially in domains where effort usually compounds. Binary uncertainty strips that intuition of its power.

Predictive horizon

Binary uncertainty is opaque at the level of individual events.

You cannot reliably predict the next outcome, no matter how many times the process has already occurred.

However, over many repetitions, proportions stabilize. Frequencies settle. The process reveals its long-run tendencies—but never its next move.

This creates a dangerous illusion: we expect short-term predictability from a structure that only offers long-term regularity.

Action implications

In binary uncertainty, action cannot shape outcomes directly.

Strategy does not tilt probabilities. Interpretation does not unlock hidden signals. Effort does not influence the mechanism itself.

The only meaningful choices concern participation: how often you engage, how much you are willing to risk, and when to stop interpreting noise as information.

Understanding this is not passive resignation. It is a form of clarity.

Knowing when outcomes are indifferent to your effort is one of the most important skills in reasoning under uncertainty.
